How my news podcast checks its own facts

Early episodes went vague whenever the morning's articles left something out. Now the writer lists what it doesn't know, and a fact-checker looks each question up before the hosts say a word.

Part of “Dutch Daily Briefing,” my daily English podcast about Dutch news→

Each episode is written from that morning’s articles. Early on, when they left something out, the hosts went vague or talked about what “the write-up” did or didn’t say. Telling the writer to be more careful only produced more hedging.

What worked was giving it a different job: for every story, list the open questions. An open question is a specific fact a curious listener would want next or a point where two outlets disagree. It has to be searchable, and anything that can’t be known yet, like a verdict that hasn’t been given, stays off the list.

  1. WriteThe briefing is written from the full articles. Each story comes with its open questions, and the text itself doesn’t hedge.
  2. Look upA fact-checker runs once per story, all at the same time. It searches the web, favoring the primary source and established outlets, often in Dutch.
  3. Answer or leave blankEvery answer needs a page the checker actually read. If it can’t confirm something, the answer stays empty.
  4. ReviseThe answers are folded into the story as normal news copy, and the script is written from that version.

So the listener gets the name, the number or the current state of a dispute where the hosts would otherwise have gone vague, and sometimes a mistake gets caught too:

What comes back

Across all the episodes so far, about six in seven questions have come back with an answer. The rest stay blank, and the hosts don’t fill the gap.

What keeps it from getting in the way

  • It can’t break the episode. Each lookup has a four-minute limit. If one fails, that story runs as written.
  • It stays off the air. The script can’t mention the research at all, so the hosts never say “we checked”.
  • Follow-ups stay short. When a story continues from an earlier episode, the checker sticks to what’s new.

The answers still vary from run to run, and some of the pages it cites are weaker than I’d like, such as Wikipedia or an outlet with an obvious slant. The briefing published with each episode lists every question with its answer and links, so I can see where a fact came from when something sounds off.
